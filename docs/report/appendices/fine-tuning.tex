\section{Clustering of the World Values Survey responses}
\label{subsec:clustering-survey}

This section records an exploratory analysis that was not used in the final methodology. We wanted to know whether the New Zealand public could be divided into value-based subpopulations, so that we could align a separate model to each subpopulation and compare the resulting alignments. This tests whether a single New Zealand-wide model hides meaningful variation between value groups. The clusters were not robust or defensible enough to support that comparison, so the final report aligns to the overall New Zealand distribution rather than to separate subpopulations. The survey's demographic quotas are a separate design choice for representativeness, not the reason for clustering. The analysis is reported here for transparency and as a starting point for a follow-up.

\subsection{What was tried}

The WVS data contain 1,057 New Zealand respondents and 251 value questions, each with a varying number of categorical options. We wanted groups of respondents with similar value responses and sufficiently different responses from other groups, and we wanted the solution to be stable across methods and resamples, because an unstable partition would make any difference between subpopulation-specific models uninterpretable.

The plan was to use these clusters as separate alignment targets: build a training set from each cluster's response distributions, fine-tune a model on each, and compare the resulting models (and the overall model) on held-out survey questions and behaviour. The pipeline therefore supports \texttt{cluster\_0}, \texttt{cluster\_1} and \texttt{overall} training sets and adapters.

We used latent class analysis (LCA) as the primary method \cite{lazarsfeldLatentStructureAnalysis1968,bakkEstimatingAssociationLatent2013,golliniMixtureLatentTrait2013}. LCA assumes that each respondent belongs to a latent class and that responses are generated by a mixture of classes; it is a standard tool for clustering survey respondents \cite{magunBetweenCountryValueDiversity2016}. Because its assumptions (local independence, homogeneity and the correct number of classes) are not fully met, we compared it with K-modes and several MCA-based methods (K-means, Ward, K-medoids, spectral, HDBSCAN and a Gaussian mixture). Solutions were compared on BIC and AIC \cite{schwarzEstimatingDimensionModel1978}, cluster-size entropy, within-cluster coherence, between-cluster total variation distance, and bootstrap stability measured by the adjusted Rand index (ARI) \cite{hubertComparingPartitions1985}.

\subsection{Results}

LCA gave the best within-cluster coherence (0.284) and between-cluster separation (mean pairwise TVD 0.194) at $k=2$, with near-even cluster sizes (entropy 0.996). K-modes was close on coherence (0.279) but its assignments differed from LCA (ARI 0.419), so the solution was sensitive to the method.

The $k=2$ solution split respondents into groups of 572 and 485. The smaller group (cluster~1) was older, more religious, less educated and poorer (\figref{clusters/k2_analysis/figures/cluster_demographics_infographic.png}). The questions that most separated the groups concerned trust in elections and the World Health Organization, homosexuality, and immigration.

The number of clusters was not clear-cut. BIC was lowest at $k=3$, but only slightly below $k=2$ (599{,}070 vs 599{,}674) and rose for $k>3$; AIC continued to fall with $k$. A bootstrap likelihood-ratio test \cite{mclachlanBootstrappingLikelihoodRatio1987,mclachlanFiniteMixtureModels2000} also slightly favoured $k=3$ ($p=0.032$). However, the $k=3$ solution was not a clean refinement of the $k=2$ solution: it reassigned respondents across both clusters (ARI between the two partitions $=0.341$) and was much less stable under the bootstrap (\tabref{cluster-summary}). The $k=2$ solution was therefore the more parsimonious and reproducible choice, but its bootstrap ARI of 0.718 is still only moderate.

Posterior certainty was high (mean 0.994; 98.2\% of respondents above 0.9), and about ten well-chosen questions were enough for confident assignment, so assigning respondents to a cluster was not the problem. The problem was the definition of the clusters: they were sensitive to the method, the number of clusters was ambiguous, and the $k=2$ solution was only moderately stable. The clusters were therefore not used as separate alignment targets in the final analysis; the final models are trained on the overall New Zealand distribution, and the survey uses demographic quotas for representativeness. The main lesson for a follow-up is that subpopulation alignment is only meaningful if the subpopulations themselves are stable and defensible.

\subsection{Training set size}
\label{subsec:training-set-size}

The WVS New Zealand sample has 1,057 respondents and 251 value items. Each
item is rendered under all eight system prompts, so one subpopulation has
$251 \times 8 = 2{,}008$ item--prompt rows. Six items are held out (Q6,
Q22, Q60, Q93, Q188 and Q215), and the \texttt{generic\_answer} prompt is
held out. Training is the complement: 245 items $\times$ 7 prompts $=
1{,}715$ rows. Validation is 251 rows under \texttt{generic\_answer} (all
items) plus 42 rows for the six held-out items under the seven seen
prompts, or 293 rows in total (\ref{tab:training-set-size}). The same
split is used for all four methods, with the letter-labelled prompt
variants for the first-token dataset; this report uses the \texttt{overall}
subpopulation.

The 1,715 rows are not 1,715 distinct questions: they contain 245 unique
items, each seen seven times per epoch (21 times over three epochs).

$K$ is the number of response options for a WVS item and is not fixed: it
ranges from 2 to 11 (mean 5.54), with 43 items at 3 options, 95 at 5, and
51 at 10--11 (10-point scales, with or without ``Don't know''). This is
separate from the fixed three-way coding used for the dilemmas in
\ref{chap:dilemmas,chap:survey} (option A, option B, \texttt{Other}). The
full-string
method expands each row into $K$ completions (9,499 sequences per epoch);
the first-token method uses one token per row (1,715 sequences per epoch).
The sampled target is drawn once with \texttt{random.seed(42)} and is fixed
across epochs.

\section{Calculation of response distributions for full option strings}

For the first-token dataset the response distribution is the renormalised first-token distribution over the valid option letters. For the full-string dataset the evaluator scores each option string by tracing it through the per-position top-20 log-probabilities returned with the completion. At each position it follows the generated token if that token continues the option string; otherwise it searches the alternative top-20 tokens for one that does. The option score is the sum of the log-probabilities along that path, exponentiated and renormalised over the valid options. This is necessary because options can share a first token (for example, ``Strongly agree'' and ``Strongly disagree''), so a first-token distribution cannot separate them.

The trace is an approximation to the exact string probability: it uses only the returned top-20 alternatives and the generated path. If the option string cannot be followed to the end, the score is the sum over the prefix that could be resolved; if no prefix is found, the function falls back to the first token's top-logprob alternatives; if no log-probabilities are available, the evaluator falls back to a one-hot distribution from the parsed answer. Training uses the exact joint log-probability of the option string in \eqref{eq:joint-logprob}, so the evaluation is an approximation to the training objective. Because different surface forms compete for probability mass, string probabilities are not calibrated across options \cite{holtzmanSurfaceFormCompetition2021}; renormalising over the valid options is the same convention used in training \eqref{eq:option-softmax}.

\section{Effect of alignment on the model capabilities}
\label{sec:capability-testing}

When fine-tuning a model it is important to ensure that capabilities do not regress elsewhere: fine-tuning can cause catastrophic forgetting of earlier tasks \cite{kirkpatrickOvercomingCatastrophicForgetting2017} and can weaken safety alignment even when the fine-tuning data are benign \cite{qiSafetyAlignmentShould2024}. As we are changing the weights it is quite plausible that the model could lose some of its knowledge or abilities. To test this we ran the base and fine-tuned models through a sample of the MMLU Pro and IFEval benchmark. The results are shown in \tabref{ft-capability-Qwen3.8-27B}. 

We can see that all but the full-string fine-tuning method maintain the same level of performance as the base model when looking at innate knowledge (MMLU Pro Direct) and instruction following (IFEval). However, all of the models show a large drop in reasoning performance. Furthermore, when strictly marking (requiring a single token followed by \texttt{<eos>}), MMLU-Pro shows a large loss for all methods except \modelref{ft-first-token}.

Given this it is clear that the fine-tuning methods have caused some negative effects on the overall model. Further testing would be needed to understand the extent of this. It suggests a need to augment the fine-tuning process to ensure maintenance of the models' capabilities (maybe smaller adapter, or interleaved training).
