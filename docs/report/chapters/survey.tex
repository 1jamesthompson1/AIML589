When aligning any AI system to a preferred value system, the decision of which value system to align with is central to the \scare{success} of the alignment. The goal of artificial intelligence is to benefit all humanity. Democratic ideals are a widely accepted basis for legitimising the world's largest institutions (governments); the same logic should apply to AI systems. The value target for the AI system should therefore be that of the public. This chapter evaluates alignment using the public's preferences and opinions, elicited through a survey.

In this chapter the most behaviourally divergent fine-tuned model (see \ref{chap:finetuning}) on the dilemmas (see \ref{chap:dilemmas}) is compared with the base model in a survey of the New Zealand public. The survey uses a quota sample designed to be representative of the New Zealand public and is designed to answer \rqref{prefer} by asking the public to compare the responses from each model to a series of dilemmas. We targeted 500 respondents, each of whom rated five pairwise comparisons. Preference is estimated with a linear mixed model, which gives the mean shift on the signed preference scale, and a Bradley–Terry model, which gives the win rate. The analysis finds a small preference for the fine-tuned model; the lack of a clear general preference is attributed to the small and noisy stimulus set (18 dilemmas). When comparing response-option distributions, the fielded model is closest to the public's inferred distribution.

The structure of the chapter is as follows. In \ref{sec:survey-construction} the survey method and question design are discussed. In \ref{sec:survey-analysis} the methods for calculating the public preference are described. Lastly, in \ref{sec:survey-results} the results of the survey are presented.

\section{Building a representative model preference survey}
\label{sec:survey-construction}

The process of public consultation is the main method of understanding the public's preferences and opinions on a topic. A survey is a strong baseline method as it allows for more detailed information to be collected than a simple poll while being cheaper and easier to scale than focus groups and interviews. Additionally, a survey collects the data in a structured way that allows for more rigorous analysis and interpretation. Therefore a survey is the method of choice to understand the public's preferences on the two models.

The survey was administered online and distributed to a representative sample of the New Zealand population. Paid respondents were recruited through the professional panel provider Dynata to ensure sufficient sample size and diversity. The median completion time was about nine minutes; respondents rated five pairwise comparisons of the two models and answered a series of questions on AI.

A key challenge in survey design is to ensure that the survey is fairly representative of the population's preferences. As we are deciding which model is preferred, there are a few different ways in which the survey could be biased. The first is that the survey is not representative of the population, the second is that the survey is not representative of the model's behaviour, and the third is that the survey design biases the results. Each of these challenges, and the design choices made to mitigate them, is discussed in the following sections.

\subsection{Using the most divergent model for the survey}

In \ref{chap:dilemmas} four different models were compared with the base model. The goal of this chapter is to determine whether one of the models is more aligned with the New Zealand public than the base model. Resource constraints meant that the survey could compare only one fine-tuned model with the base model. It was also important to select the model before conducting sophisticated analyses of model behaviour, to avoid selection bias or reliance on potentially biased LLM-as-judge scores. The selection was therefore based only on the categorical response-divergence measure, not on the judge's quality scores.

The goal of the survey is to determine which of the two models people prefer. Under the assumption that the base model is not well aligned with New Zealand values, we chose the model that differs most from it. This increases the difference in responses and therefore the power to detect a difference between the models, if one exists. The null hypothesis is that there is no preference; choosing the most divergent model increases statistical power by increasing the effect size. The model that is most divergent from the base model is \modelref{ft-modal}, which is therefore used in the survey.

\subsection{Creating a fair and representative survey}

The survey can only be as representative as its respondents. Representativeness has two dimensions: demographic and value. We want a wide range of people who represent different demographics but also different ways of thinking within New Zealand. Because the value space is hard to measure and sample from, a representative demographic sample is used as a proxy for value representativeness. This is supported by evidence that demographic groups differ systematically in their values and opinions \cite{santurkarWhoseOpinionsLanguage2023,wightmanPeopleAreNot2026,magunBetweenCountryValueDiversity2016}.

To achieve a representative sample, we used quota sampling: we set quotas for each variable and recruited participants until each quota was filled. The more quotas, the harder the sample is to fill. Because of limited resources, we limited quotas to three variables—age, gender, and region—which are standard baseline quotas for representative sampling \cite{statsnz2023gss,satherley2023polling}. These variables are also associated with systematic differences in values and opinions \cite{wightmanPeopleAreNot2026,magunBetweenCountryValueDiversity2016}. Income, education, and ethnicity were not quota-controlled because of budget and feasibility constraints.

The WVS, from which the training data originated, is intended to be a representative sample of the New Zealand population. However, as \figref{top5-wvs-vs-census} shows, it is not perfectly representative: it skews older, poorer and more female. We therefore set the quotas to the 2023 Census which is the gold standard. The WVS is an example of volunteer non-response bias, which produces a non-representative sample.

\cfigure{top5-wvs-vs-census}{The WVS survey vs the census across some common demographic variables. Notably Ethnicity is not present in the WVS survey.}

Quotas were set to match the census for each variable. Age (in five-year bands) and gender were interlocking, with an additional quota for region, creating 36 quota cells. The quotas matched the 2023 Census data provided by Dynata. Additional demographic questions—income, education, ethnicity, employment status, and occupation—were asked to assess representativeness. The achieved sample was broadly representative on the quota variables and somewhat representative on the other variables (see \ref{sec:survey-sample-details}).


Self-selection bias is an additional concern: people who choose to be on survey panels are not representative of the population. This may not affect the demographic quotas but could affect value representativeness. The panel provider draws respondents from multiple sources to reduce this risk \cite{boomImportanceMultisourcingRepresentativeness2023}.

\subsection{Selection of comparisons where the models disagree}

Each of the 30 dilemmas was run 50 times through each model. Because the full cross-product of runs is combinatorially large, it was not possible to show all 50 runs for each model. We therefore selected a subset of the possible pairwise comparisons. We wanted a subset that tests the interesting differences between the models while still representing the model's behaviour.

If the models agree, a public preference between near-identical responses tells us little. We therefore focus on the subspace where the models behave differently and disagree. Simply picking the most interesting pairs would lack any guarantee of representativeness. At the other extreme, a random sample would be representative—within sampling noise—but would include many pairs where the judge classifies both responses the same.

From \ref{chap:dilemmas} we have a measure of the quality of each index-matched pairwise comparison. Selecting the most divergent pairs within each significant dilemma preserves coverage at the dilemma level: the final set contains all 18 significant dilemmas. There is a loss of representativeness within a dilemma sample however that is tolerable given we are trying to only look at that precise subspace. The process is as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Pair each of the 50 runs of each model with one run of the other model (using the sample index). Some pairing is necessary; reusing a sample in multiple comparisons would induce dependence between comparisons. This gives a total of 1,500 pairwise comparisons across the 30 dilemmas.
    \item Remove dilemmas where the two models do not have statistically significant differences in their response distributions. This removed 12 dilemmas and left 18 dilemmas with 900 pairwise comparisons.
    \item For each dilemma, select the 17 index-matched pairs with the highest value divergence. See the power analysis for reasoning \fixme{Add the power analysis subsection (\texttt{subsec:power}); it does not exist yet.} \footnote{This is less representative of model behaviour, but the survey is testing whether the divergence is in the right direction.}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Eliciting preferences with paired comparisons}

The main goal of the survey was to elicit the public's preferences between the two models, to answer \rqref{prefer}. Two elicitation methods were possible: absolute rating and pairwise comparison. We used pairwise comparison because it is common in post-training literature \cite{ouyangTrainingLanguageModels2022,rafailovDirectPreferenceOptimization2024,zhengJudgingLLMasaJudgeMTBench2023}.

The survey showed participants five pairwise comparison blocks. Each block presented a one-line introduction to the work profile, the dilemma and question, and the two responses from the fine-tuned and base models. The order of the two responses was randomised so that presentation order is balanced.

For each comparison, participants answered two five-point Likert items, as seen in \figref{survey-ui}. The five-point scale strikes a compromise between the simplicity of a binary choice and the complexity of a ten-point scale. The first item asked which response they preferred; the second asked how strongly they agreed with an AI agent being deployed in that situation to make decisions like the ones they had just seen.

\cfigure{survey-ui}{The user interface for the preference elicitation questions. This is how it was shown on desktop with slight modifications for mobile.}

\subsection{Understanding respondents views on AI and the world}

To maximise the value of the data, we added questions that both increase interpretability of the results and capture the public's views on AI. The first question was adapted from the NZ AI election scorecard \cite{AIElectionScorecard} and asks respondents whether they view AI as a serious risk to New Zealand; AI is also increasingly discussed as a global catastrophic risk \cite{GlobalCatastrophicRisks,moonGlobalCatastrophicRisk2024}. The second asked how often they use AI in their daily life. The third asked about their outlook on the effects of AI on the future. These questions let us test whether views on AI correlate with preferences for the models.

Finally, participants selected their top three preferred AI policy responses and ranked them. The policy list was drawn from prior research by Ben Cravens (unpublished Master's thesis). We aimed to make the policies easy to read while preserving their substance. \fixme{Add a citation or personal communication for the Cravens thesis.}

The data provide additional insight into the public's perception of AI, which is useful for policy makers and researchers. A full analysis is left for future work; the appendix reports the main results.



\section{Analysis of the survey results}
\label{sec:survey-analysis}

The analysis addresses \rqref{prefer} by estimating which model's responses the public prefers. The primary, pre-registered analysis compares the two models directly \cite{thompsonPreregistrationAligningLLMs2026/10/1515:17}. As a secondary analysis, we compare each model's answer-option distribution with the public's inferred preferred-option distribution. This helps interpret the small and specification-dependent preference result.

For all methods, what random effects are included decide the scope of the claim. The pre-registration call for a respondent effect however it is observed to be near-zero. Each respondent rates multiple comparisons, and the comparisons are nested within a small set of dilemmas. A respondent random intercept and a fixed order term should therefore always be included; we also include a comparison-pair random intercept (17 pairs per dilemma). Respondent and comparison effects together answer the scoped question, ``Does the public prefer the model on these exact dilemmas?''. Adding a dilemma random intercept asks the broader question, ``Does the public prefer the model's behaviour in general?'' as the dilemmas are treated as simply a sample from the full possible set of dilemmas/situations.

The survey produces a dataset of $n=2{,}335$ ratings from $N=467$ respondents on $q=18$ dilemmas. Each rating $i$ has attached to it:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $y_i \in \{-2,-1,0,1,2\}$ for the signed preference (positive = fine-tune),
    \item $w_i \in \{0, 0.5, 1\}$ for the win (1 = fine-tune wins, 0 = base wins, 0.5 = tie),
    \item $o_i = \pm\tfrac12$ for presentation order (positive = fine-tune first),
    \item $r_i, d_i, c_i$ for the respondent, dilemma, and comparison pair,
\end{itemize}

The models also include random intercepts for the respondent, dilemma, and comparison pair ($u_r, v_d, s_c$). The random intercepts account for the variability in the data due to these grouping factors.

\subsection{Weighting of the survey results}

The survey was quota-sampled and is roughly representative of the population on age, gender, and region. However, this does not guarantee representativeness on other demographics. After the quality exclusions, the sample differs only slightly from the census. Initial testing showed that weighting made negligible difference to the results, so all reported estimates are unweighted.

\subsection{Calculating the preference using a Linear Mixed Model Analysis}

The first approach uses the Likert preference ratings to estimate whether there is an overall preference for one model over the other. A naive approach is to take the mean rating, but this ignores the random effects discussed above.

The repeated measures and the random effects of dilemmas, comparisons, and respondents must therefore be accounted for. We use a linear mixed model (LMM), which includes both fixed and random effects \cite{batesFittingLinearMixedEffects2015}. The model can also include a fixed display order effect; including it changes the aggregate estimate by less than 0.01 points, although the order term itself is large for individual ratings (see \ref{sec:order-effect}).

\begin{align}
\label{eq:lmm}
y_i &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 o_i + u_{r(i)} + v_{d(i)} + s_{c(i)} + \epsilon_i,\\
u_r &\sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_u^2),
\qquad
v_d \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_v^2),
\qquad
s_c \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_s^2),
\qquad
\epsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2).
\end{align}
\noindent Here, $\beta_0$ is the mean signed preference, which is what we care about, and $\beta_1$ is the fixed effect of display order. The model ignores differences in distance between Likert categories (e.g., $1 \to 2$ is not the same as $2 \to 3$), and ordinal models have therefore been proposed \cite{taylorRatingNormsShould2023}. However, these methods do not appreciably change the results, so we use the simpler model.

We estimate the model by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). REML is used because ML variance-component estimates are biased downward in small samples \cite{pattersonRecoveryInterblockInformation1971}.

\subsection{Using model win rates to understand preferences}

Rather than using the signed preference ratings, we can also use the pairwise win rates to estimate which model is preferred. The win rate is a more interpretable measure of preference than the mean signed preference, as it is bounded between 0 and 1. A win rate of 0.5 indicates no preference, while a win rate of 1 indicates that the fine-tuned model is always preferred.
The first method (pre-registered) is a simple clustered win rate, defined in \eqref{eq:bt-naive} as the weighted number of wins divided by the total number of comparisons. To calculate the confidence interval, we use a clustered bootstrap: we resample respondents with replacement, calculate the win rate for each bootstrap sample, and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution. Ties are given a weight of 0.5.

\begin{equation}
  \hat{p} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i w_i,
  \label{eq:bt-naive}
\end{equation}

\noindent A more sophisticated method is to fit a Bradley–Terry model with random effects for respondents, dilemmas, and comparison pairs \cite{bradleyRANKANALYSISINCOMPLETE1952}. The model is fitted as a binomial generalised linear mixed model (GLMM) with a logit link function using a Laplace approximation \cite{breslowApproximateInferenceGeneralized1993}. The model is specified in \eqref{eq:bt}.

\begin{align}
  \operatorname{logit} P(w_i = 1) &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 o_i + u_{r(i)} + v_{d(i)} + s_{c(i)}, \label{eq:bt}\\
  \hat{p} &= \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-\beta_0)}.
\end{align}

\noindent $\hat{p}$ is the predicted win probability and $\exp(\beta_0)$ is the fine-tune-to-base worth ratio.


\subsection{Comparing the model distributions to the public distribution}

We can compare answer-option distributions, as in the behavioural analysis in \ref{chap:dilemmas}. Under the assumption that preferring a response is a vote for its judged answer category, the pairwise ratings imply a distribution over the public's preferred answer options. We can then compare this distribution with each model's answer-option distribution to see which model is closer. We use the total variation distance (TVD). For each dilemma $d$, let $p_{dk}$ be the public's share for answer option $k$ and $q_{dk}$ the model's share. Then
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{TVD}_d = \frac{1}{2}\sum_k \left| p_{dk} - q_{dk} \right|,
  \label{eq:tvd}
\end{equation}
which ranges from 0 (identical distributions) to 1 (no overlap).

This analysis relies on the assumption that the public's preferred option can be measured by the preference for each model in the pairwise comparisons. Each response was classified into an answer category by the judge, so a preference for one response over the other can be treated as a vote for that category. Ties are split equally between the two options. The catch-all \texttt{Other} category is rare overall (1.4–3.3\% of runs) but reaches 28\% in some dilemmas. It is included in the distributions, but it is not one of the two substantive options.

With the public's inferred preferred-option distribution, we compare it with each model's answer-option distribution. To construct each model's distribution, we use all 50 runs per dilemma, which gives a more stable estimate of model behaviour. We do not restrict the model distribution to the 17 selected pairs, because the public's votes are assumed to be independent draws from its option distribution \footnote{This assumes that all of the model's responses are equally fair representatives of its assigned answer option.}.

\section{Results of the survey}
\label{sec:survey-results}

The first question is whether the public prefers one model over another. Two scopes are possible: ``Does the public prefer the model on these exact dilemmas?'' (exploratory) or, more broadly, ``Does the public prefer the model's behaviour in general?'' (pre-registered). The scoped claim is statistically significant, but there is too much between-dilemma variance to support a general claim.

The appendix reports the public's policy preferences and their relationship to views on AI (\ref{sec:survey-policy-preferences}), and the demographic analysis (\ref{sec:survey-demographics}).

\subsection{The public's preference is messy and inconclusive}

To answer \rqref{prefer}, two analyses were pre-registered: a linear mixed model on the Likert ratings and a Bradley–Terry model on the pairwise preferences.

\tabref{survey-h2-results} reports the pre-registered estimates; \tabref{survey-h2-lmm-variants} and \tabref{survey-h2-bt-variants} show the sensitivity to the random-effects structure.

We distinguish two claims. The scoped claim—``Does the public prefer the model on these exact dilemmas?''—corresponds to a model without a dilemma random effect. The scoped BT model, a GLMM on decided comparisons with ties excluded, estimates a 54.8\% win rate (95\% CI 51.5–58.0\%), and the scoped LMM estimates a +0.11-point shift (95\% CI 0.03–0.19). This supports the scoped claim that the public prefers the fine-tuned model on these exact dilemmas.

For the broader pre-registered claim—``Does the public prefer the model's behaviour in general?''—we find no significant preference for either model. The stimulus set contains only 18 dilemmas, a small sample from the space of possible dilemmas. The between-dilemma variance is too large for an informative general claim.

Looking at each dilemma individually in \figref{survey-dilemma-preference}, some show strong preferences for one model over the other. The direction is roughly balanced: 10 of 18 dilemmas have a positive mean and 8 negative, so the aggregate preference is minimal. This heterogeneity may reflect context-dependent preferences, but it may also reflect the different respondent subsets who saw each dilemma. This large between-dilemma variance is why the broader claim is inconclusive: the 18-dilemma frame is too small to support a claim about the model's behaviour in general.
